Probe placement — what the three options actually change

All four panels rasterise the same curved surface against the same cell lattice with the same barycentric sampling. The only thing that differs is where the probe ends up and when a cell counts as already covered. Panel 1 is what Frontier ships today.

How to read this — and one result that surprised me

Watch min spacing, measured in cells. It is the number that matters, because today's rule bounds probe density (one per cell) but not probe spacing — two probes in adjacent cells can both sit against their shared boundary and end up almost touching.

Option 1 is the clear winner on spacing, and it is not close. At the default settings the shipping rule's closest pair is 0.024 of a cell apart; snapping takes that to 0.215. Drag Curvature to 0 and snapping reaches 1.000 — exactly one cell, perfectly lattice-locked, which is the uniformity you saw in the prototype.

Option 2 is worse than option 1 on spacing, which I did not expect until I measured it. Adding the normal-cone to the key subdivides each cell into up to 6 independent buckets (4 in this cross-section), so several probes are now allowed to share one cell by design — min spacing falls back to 0.039, and on a flat surface two probes can land 0.000 apart. That is the correct behaviour for a crease (a wall and a floor meeting in one cell genuinely want two probes) but it is not what you asked for, and it partially undoes option 1's gain. Turn on Mark too-close pairs and compare panels 2 and 3 directly.

Turn on Uneven tessellation and drag Tessellation up to see the Suzanne case: where triangles are tiny, many fight over one cell and the winner is arbitrary, so panel 1's probes scatter within their cells while panel 2's sit on the lattice regardless of how the mesh is cut.

The trade-off in option 3 I want to flag before you pick

Option 3 adds the prototype's CellEdgeAt(distance) power-of-two LOD. Frontier's grid already does distance LOD via its surfel cascades (SurfelGridCoordToCascadeFloat → SurfelCascadeFloatToCascade, already read by SampleStepsForTriangle). Adding a second distance term on top would coarsen twice — the far field would thin out roughly quadratically instead of linearly. If you want option 3, the honest version is reconciling the two into one LOD, not stacking them, and that is a bigger change than options 1 or 2.
