The two slow passes — what they are & why they dominate

Rendering one frame runs a chain of GPU passes. Two of them eat almost all the time: Integrate and Shade. The others (slot, spawn, age) are rounding error. Here is what each one actually does, in plain terms.

1 · One frame, drawn to scale (measured GPU ms)

The width of each block IS its cost. The two coloured blocks are ~95% of the frame. Make either one cheaper and the whole frame gets faster; touching the grey blocks does nothing.

2 · What each pass is, in one sentence

🔴 INTEGRATE — “gather light for every surfel”
SurfelIntegrate.comp · 1 GPU lane per surfel

A surfel is a little disc of surface stuck to the world that remembers how much light lands on it. Integrate is the pass that updates that memory. For every surfel, it shoots a fistful of rays into the scene to ask “what light is coming at me from all directions?”, averages the answers, and blends the result into the surfel’s stored value.

This is the cost of computing the global illumination (bounced light) itself.

  • Fire ~8–32 rays into the hemisphere (SLG-guided)
  • Each ray traces the BVH to find what it hit
  • At each hit: albedo × sun light — and a sun-shadow ray
  • Plus a one-bounce gather reading neighbour surfels
  • Fold the frame’s estimate into stored moments (temporal average)
🟡 SHADE — “colour every pixel on screen”
SurfaceShade.frag · 1 GPU lane per screen pixel

Shade is the pass that paints the final image you see. For every pixel, it figures out which surface is there, looks up the nearby surfels’ stored light (the GI from Integrate), adds the direct sun, and writes the colour. This is where the sun’s hard shadows get traced.

This is the cost of displaying the lighting, not computing the bounce.

  • Decode which surface & material this pixel is (visibility buffer)
  • Look up surfel GI around the point
  • Add direct sun — fire N sun-shadow rays across the sun disc
  • Combine, tonemap, write the pixel

“view-varying” = its cost changes with the camera — more visible shadowed surface ⇒ more shadow rays ⇒ 7 ms one frame, 11 ms the next.

3 · Why THESE two, and not the others

PassRuns once per…Rays it firesCost
slot / spawn / agesurfel (bookkeeping only)none — just sorts & counters~noise
Integratesurfel (tens of thousands)8–32 bounce + 1 sun each8.2 ms
Shadepixel (millions)N sun-shadow each7–11 ms

The pattern: the slow passes are the ones that trace rays. Ray tracing walks a tree of the whole scene per ray — that’s hundreds of steps. Slot/spawn/age only shuffle numbers, so they’re free by comparison. Integrate is slow because there are many surfels × many rays. Shade is slow because there are millions of pixels, each firing sun rays.

4 · Why the shadow plan attacks this problem

Both hot passes fire sun-shadow rays. Integrate fires one per bounce hit; Shade fires N per pixel. So the any-hit + adaptive-penumbra work isn’t a side quest — it directly shrinks both red and amber blocks at once. exact · no quality loss

But shadows aren’t the whole story. Integrate’s biggest cost is the 8–32 bounce rays and the moment-folding — the shadow ray is just one line inside that loop. So the shadow plan is step one; the deeper Integrate win is fewer / cheaper bounce rays (temporal reuse, ray reduction) — that’s the part still fully open.

TL;DR — Integrate = compute the bounced light (per surfel, many rays). Shade = paint it to the screen (per pixel, sun-shadow rays). Both are ray-bound, which is why they’re slow and why shadow-ray optimisation hits both. Bounce-ray reduction in Integrate is the next frontier.
